# Cognitive rhythm

Dense prose is not boring because it holds too much.
It is boring when every paragraph runs in the same cognitive mode.
A reader observes, hesitates, becomes sure, checks again;
a text that never switches between those gives them no reason to keep going,
however correct it is.

The pull comes from one thing: **an unresolved tension**,
always at least one open — a question not yet answered,
a certainty not yet checked, an answer promised for later.
The moment every tension is closed, the reader can stop, and does.
These rules come from editorial practice and rest on no standard.

They apply to prose read in order — a chapter, an article, an essay,
an explanation.
A page that is scanned (a README, a procedure, a reference) belongs to `document-structure`,
whose rule is the opposite: conclusion first.
One document gets one of the two, never both.

## Language layers

The rule is language-neutral; what differs by language is layered on,
not mixed in.
Japanese: read `references/japanese.md` before writing or revising Japanese.
It carries the forms that exist only there and worked examples in Japanese.
A draft in another language uses the body alone.

## The moments this replaces

| About to… | Instead |
|---|---|
| open with an agenda ("this article covers A, B and C") | make one tension in the first few sentences: restate the reader's own experience and hand it to a hypothesis, state a confident general claim the text will test, write the narrator's belief and let the facts break it, restate the question the last chapter left |
| open a section with "in this section we cover" | restate the unease the previous section left as the narrator's own question; write the objection the reader would raise; begin with an admission |
| end a section with "next we look at" | delete it. The bridge goes at the head of the next section; a section that opens with a question needs no forecast before it |
| introduce a theory, a concept or a quotation, then confirm it with an example | make the reader feel a problem that has no name yet, then bring the theory in as its name |
| write a third long assertion in a row | plant a short sentence, run a longer one, stop on a short one; put a hesitation ("it must have been fine", "or was it") between the assertions |
| list properties and move on | land each one on the concrete scene just shown, and vary how each lands |
| write about this text ("so far this may look like a description", "what this section is really about is") | delete it. If the logic then skips, rewrite it as a sentence about the subject ("all three of these are in the failure at the start") |
| declare a device ("let me give half the answer first") | write the first half of the answer as content. A device that works is invisible |
| close every question and end on a rule | land the abstraction on something the reader already holds (the opening scene, their own experience) and leave one question open |
| cut context to make the beat | do not. The sentences that share scope, viewpoint and the axis of comparison are not slack; removing them is omission |

## Does the sentence update the situation, or the document?

Slack and dead prose look alike.
One test separates them.

- A sentence that **updates the situation** tells the reader something new about the world of the subject (an event, a figure, what someone said) or about the narrator's state of judgement:
  a belief, a reservation, a regret, a concession, an admission.
  It may stay as slack.
- A sentence that **updates the document** says only how this chapter,
  this section, the argument so far *looks*, or what comes next.
  It goes.

> It must have been working.
> Nothing needs doing right now,
> though at some point it will want tidying up.

Both update the narrator's judgement: a belief about to be overturned,
a deferral made visible.

> So far this may look like a description of a concept,
> so let me get back to the example.
> This is not a list of techniques.
> The only question is X.
> Next we look at observation.

All three are about the text and carry nothing about the subject.
Short does not save them, nor does the negative,
nor a confident tone. **The commonest way dead prose survives is being cut down to a punchy line**;
good rhythm is not a reason to keep a sentence.

Four forms that talk about the document may stay:

1. **Handling an objection**,
   quoting the misreading it rejects ("if this reads as 'always do X', that is not it").
   "Don't misunderstand me" on its own is dead.
2. **Setting and collecting a question**:
   "this chapter asks…" after the tension exists,
   and "that is half the answer".
3. **A request to the reader**, at a boundary: "read this as…".
4. **Opening and closing an example's frame**: "suppose that…",
   "back to the example at the start".

The procedure: delete first and read across the gap.
If it reads, done.
If the logic skips, rewrite as a sentence about the subject;
if the rewrite still talks about the text, the rewrite failed.

## Keep one tension open

The opening's job is to make one, in any of several shapes:
the reader's own experience restated and handed to a hypothesis;
a confident general claim the rest will test;
the narrator's belief written sincerely, then broken by the facts;
the last section's question in the narrator's words.
A preview with an attitude ("there is no better way in than this") makes a tension of its own;
only the agenda without one is forbidden.

Questions raised are collected, and collected visibly.
Returning an answer in halves pulls the second half of the piece.
When the draft is done, build a **ledger of tensions**: every question,
belief and promise in the text, and the line where each is collected.
One that points nowhere is either collected by a new sentence or cut with its question.

The close lands the abstraction on something the reader already holds (the opening scene, their own experience, the early question) and only then stops.
Tensions are closed by choice, and one may stay open at the end.

## Beat and wave

A paragraph's base beat is plant, run, stop:
a short sentence for footing, a longer one that carries,
a short one that halts.
Assertion alone flattens; alternate it with hesitation.
A belief that the facts will later break ("it must have been working") steers the reader's prediction before it is overturned.
At a turn, concession, turn, short halt:

> That will do.
> It will probably go on doing.
> But what matters here is the other case.
> That is the one to think about.

Density waves too.
After two or three dense paragraphs, one sparse one,
whose only jobs are to fix a settled point in a line,
to name the next thing under judgement,
or to change the viewing distance.
Paragraphs close to the material (records, numbers, quotations, code) alternate with paragraphs that step back to interpret.
A bulleted list is a pause in the breathing as much as a compression,
and the sentence that steps back after it works because of the pause.

## A section enters on a question, an unease or an admission

Not on "in this section we cover".
Restate the unease the previous section left, in the narrator's voice.
Or write the reader's objection as it stands ("then should we have done X first?") — and do not answer at once;
concede ("we would have liked to") before breaking it.
Or admit something; the admission is not self-criticism but footing for the argument that follows ("that plan was half right").

Theory, concepts and quotations come after the reader has an unease with no name,
and arrive as its name.
Theory first, then an example to confirm it, takes the discovery away.

The bridge between sections is the head of the next one,
not the foot of the last.
"Next we look at" is commentary on the text's progress;
a section that opens on an objection needs no forecast.

A list of properties lands each item on the concrete scene just shown ("the first is what we saw in the failure"; "the second will be familiar"),
and the landings do not all take the same form.

## Perform the device; take the material from the situation

When these rules are applied to new sentences, the beat,
the tension and the slack take their material only from the situation:
the events, data and voices of the subject,
and the narrator's judgement.
In an explanation with no scene,
the situation is the subject's own nature (the data, the calculation, the trade-off, the naive expectation the facts break) and the reader's own inferences and objections.
Where no material exists, add nothing; leave it flat.

The names of the devices do not appear in the text.
"Return the answer in halves" is performed by writing the first half.
"Let me give half the answer first",
"one more line under this at the end" — a sentence that announces the operation about to be performed is itself dead.

## After the draft

1. **Topic test.** Take every paragraph's first sentence and every short standalone sentence and ask:
   situation or document?
   Sentences written during revision, and long ones cut into short ones,
   are the usual route in; test them as they are written.
2. **Leak test.** Search the text for the vocabulary of these rules (tension, collect, half the answer) and for "next we look at" at every section's foot.
3. **Ledger.** List the questions, beliefs and promises,
   and point at each one's collection.
4. **Beat.** Find three long assertions in a row and break them with a footing,
   a halt or a hesitation.
5. **Boundaries.** Second person,
   requests and self-deprecation belong at the opening and the close;
   move or cut any found in the middle.

Symptoms map to remedies.
Every paragraph the same and tiring: no beat.
Correct but no wish to read on: no tension.
The theory section goes cold: theory before unease.
The close preaches: it ended on an abstraction.
The opening is clerical: an agenda without attitude.

## What this is not for

A preview with an attitude.
Second person, a request or self-deprecation at a boundary.
The sentence that opens or closes an example's frame.
An objection quoted and rejected.
The context-sharing sentences at the start.
Flatness where there is no material.

## Connections

Pages that are scanned: `document-structure`,
whose conclusion-first rule is the opposite of this one.
The kind of document decides which applies.
